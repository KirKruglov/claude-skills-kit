# Job Description and Scorecard Builder — User Guide

Learn how to generate a job description and interview scorecard from your role notes in one pass.

---

## Quick Start

Here's the fastest way to get your hiring documents:

1. Write 3–5 sentences about the role: title, level, and 2–3 must-have requirements
2. Say: "Write a job description for [role]" and paste your notes
3. Get back a complete Job Description and a matched Interview Scorecard

**Result:** Two copy-paste-ready documents — post the JD, print the scorecard.

**Time:** ~3 minutes

---

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Opening a New Role Without a Job Description

**Situation:**
You are a team lead at a growing startup. Your team needs a backend engineer, but you've never written a formal job description before. Your recruiter is handling sourcing, but expects you to define the role requirements. You have 30 minutes before your next meeting.

**What to do:**

1. Jot down your role requirements in a message
   - Include at minimum: title, level, and 3–4 things the candidate absolutely must have
   - Example: "Backend Engineer, Senior, must have: 4+ years Go or Python, REST APIs, PostgreSQL, experience with high-traffic systems. Nice to have: Kubernetes. Remote. Team of 5 engineers."

2. Trigger the skill by saying: "Write a job description for this role"
   - Paste your notes
   - The skill will extract the structured fields and flag anything missing

3. Review the two documents
   - **Job Description:** check that the Responsibilities section reflects what the person will actually do; add or remove bullets as needed
   - **Interview Scorecard:** verify that each must-have requirement has a matching criterion with anchors

4. Use the output
   - Share the JD with your recruiter or post it directly
   - Print or paste the Scorecard into your interview notes template

**Expected result:**

You receive a structured Job Description with About the Role, Responsibilities, Requirements, and Compensation sections — and an Interview Scorecard where every must-have requirement maps to a rated criterion with 1–4 behavioral anchors.

**Why this works:** Instead of starting from a blank template, you describe the role in plain language and get consistent, professionally structured documents in minutes. The scorecard is built directly from the JD, so your evaluation criteria match what you advertised.

### Scenario 2: Running a Hiring Loop as a Non-Technical Founder

**Situation:**
You are a founder hiring your first sales hire — a Head of Sales. You've never evaluated salespeople before, and you want to make sure each interviewer is scoring candidates on the same criteria. You need a JD to post on LinkedIn and a scorecard to share with the 3 people doing interviews.

**What to do:**

1. Describe the role from what you know
   - Include the level, what the role will own, and your must-haves
   - Example: "Head of Sales, Senior. Must have: 5+ years in B2B SaaS sales, track record of building a sales team, experience closing $50k+ ACV deals. Nice to have: startup experience. On-site NYC. Reports to me (CEO). Comp: $120–150k + equity."

2. Say: "Job description and scorecard for this role"
   - Paste the notes
   - Get back both documents in one response

3. Share with your interview panel
   - Send the JD to your recruiter
   - Send the Scorecard to the 3 interviewers with instructions: "Rate each criterion 1–4 after your session. We'll compare scores before deciding."

4. Use scores for calibration
   - After all interviews, collect the scorecards
   - Compare criterion-by-criterion to identify where interviewers agree or diverge
   - Make the final decision based on averaged scores + individual notes

**Expected result:**

You receive a complete JD ready for LinkedIn and a Scorecard with 5–7 criteria (one per must-have), each with behavioral anchors calibrated for a Head of Sales role. All three interviewers use the same scoring rubric, making it easy to compare candidates objectively.

**Why this works:** As a non-technical founder hiring for a specialized role, a consistent scorecard prevents gut-feel bias and gives you evidence-based conversations with co-interviewers. Time saved: 1–2 hours vs. building the rubric from scratch.

### Scenario 3: Updating an Existing Job Description

**Situation:**
You posted a JD for a Marketing Manager three months ago. The role didn't fill, and after reviewing applications, you realize the requirements were too broad. You want to tighten the must-haves, add a level, and update the compensation to reflect current market rates.

**What to do:**

1. Paste the existing JD into the chat
   - Include the full text of the original job description

2. Say: "Update this JD with these changes" and list your updates
   - Example: "Change level to Mid-level. Add 'experience with email marketing automation (HubSpot or Mailchimp)' to Must Have. Remove 'agency experience' from Must Have — move it to Nice to Have. Update compensation to $80–100k."

3. Review the updated documents
   - The skill will flag which sections changed and keep the rest intact
   - Check that the Scorecard's criteria reflect the new must-haves

4. Re-post the updated JD

**Expected result:**

You receive an updated JD with your changes applied and a note: "Updated: Requirements (Must Have, Nice to Have), Compensation. Unchanged: About the Role, Responsibilities, About the Team." The Scorecard is regenerated to match the new must-have list.

**Why this works:** Instead of editing the JD manually and then updating the scorecard separately, one message applies changes to both documents simultaneously and keeps them in sync.

## Tips

### Tip 1: Lead With Must-Haves — They Drive the Scorecard

The must-have requirements you provide are the most important input. Each one becomes a scored criterion in the scorecard. If you include vague must-haves (e.g., "strong communicator"), you'll get vague scorecard anchors. Be specific: "ability to run 1-on-1s with direct reports and give structured feedback" generates much more useful evaluation criteria.

**Pro tip:** Aim for 3–6 must-haves. More than 8 makes the scorecard unwieldy; fewer than 3 makes it hard to differentiate candidates.

### Tip 2: Use the Scorecard After Every Interview — Not Just at the End

Don't score candidates from memory at the end of a hiring loop. Fill in the scorecard immediately after each interview, while the conversation is fresh. A score of 3 given right after the call is more reliable than a 3 reconstructed a week later.

**Pro tip:** Add a "Key Evidence" column to the scorecard mentally — when you write a 4 for a criterion, jot one sentence on what the candidate said or did that earned that score. This makes debrief conversations much more productive.

### Tip 3: Share the JD and Scorecard Together With Your Interview Panel

Before your panel starts interviewing, send both documents at once. Interviewers who read the JD and scorecard together understand what "Meets bar" looks like for each criterion — reducing calibration variance across interviewers.

**Pro tip:** Hold a 15-minute calibration call with the panel before the first interview. Walk through 2–3 criteria and agree on what a "3" vs. a "4" looks like. This investment pays off when you debrief candidates.
