# Accounts Receivable Follow-up Writer — User Guide

Learn how to get a professional, relationship-aware invoice reminder sequence in minutes.

---

## Quick Start

Here's the fastest way to get your reminder sequence:

1. Note your client name, invoice amount, days overdue, and one-word relationship description (e.g., "long-term", "new", "difficult")
2. Say: "Write invoice reminder for [Client], [Amount], [X] days overdue, [relationship]"
3. Review the 4-message sequence and personalize before sending

**Result:** A complete escalating sequence — from gentle nudge to final notice — ready to adapt and send.

**Time:** ~3 minutes

## Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Freelancer Following Up on a First Late Invoice from a Good Client

**Situation:**
You are a freelance web designer. You completed a project last month and invoiced your client — a small agency you've worked with for two years — for $4,500. They've always paid on time, so 21 days of silence feels unusual. You don't want to damage the relationship with a heavy-handed message, but you need the money. You want a series of messages that starts warm and escalates only if needed.

**What to do:**

1. Trigger the skill with your details
   - Say: "Write invoice reminder sequence for [Agency Name], $4,500, 21 days overdue, long-term client who usually pays on time"
   - Or more simply: "My client hasn't paid. It's been 21 days. We've worked together for 2 years."

2. Review the generated sequence
   - The skill will start at **Message 2 (Neutral)** given 21 days overdue with a long-term relationship
   - Check the tone notes under each message — they explain *why* each message is phrased that way
   - Read the relationship risk flag (⚠️) if it appears before Messages 3 or 4

3. Personalize and stage the messages
   - Add your actual invoice number and due date
   - Adjust the opening ("Hi Sarah," vs. "Dear Ms. Smith") to match your normal communication style
   - Set calendar reminders for the recommended send dates

**Expected result:**

You receive 4 messages covering the full arc — from a friendly check-in to a formal final notice — each with a subject line, body, recommended send timing, and tone explanation. You send Message 2 today, with Messages 3 and 4 staged as drafts for later if needed.

**Why this works:** Instead of crafting each message from scratch (and second-guessing every word), you get a coherent sequence designed for escalation. You keep full control of what you actually send — the skill just removes the blank-page problem.

### Scenario 2: Small Business Owner with a Silent New Client

**Situation:**
You run a marketing consultancy. A new client signed a contract three months ago and paid a 50% deposit upfront. The project is done, but the remaining $6,000 final invoice has gone unpaid for 47 days. You've sent one email reminder (no response) and tried calling once (voicemail). You're not sure what's going on and want a firm-but-professional series that doesn't burn bridges in case there's a legitimate delay.

**What to do:**

1. Provide the full context
   - Say: "Invoice follow-up for new client, $6,000, 47 days overdue. Already sent one email two weeks ago, called once, no response."
   - The skill uses your prior contact history to advance the starting level

2. Review the starting point
   - Given 47 days overdue + prior unanswered contact, the skill starts at **Message 3 (Firm)**
   - Read the ⚠️ relationship risk note — it will suggest one last direct outreach attempt before escalating to Message 4

3. Decide your escalation timeline
   - Use the recommended send dates (e.g., Message 3 now, Message 4 in 10–14 days if no response)
   - If the client responds after Message 3, you can stop the sequence

**Expected result:**

You receive Messages 3 and 4 immediately usable, with Message 4 including a note about involving collections. The skill flags that a phone call after Message 3 may resolve the issue faster than email.

**Why this works:** The skill adapts to the situation — it doesn't give you a "gentle reminder" when you're already 47 days in with no response. You get a sequence that matches where you actually are in the escalation arc.

### Scenario 3: Account Manager Preparing a Sequence in Advance

**Situation:**
You are an account manager at a SaaS company. You have 5 enterprise clients with invoices due in the next 30 days. Company policy is to begin follow-up at Day 15 past due. Rather than drafting 5 separate email series when each invoice goes overdue, you want to prepare customized reminder sequences for each client now, so you can send them quickly when the time comes.

**What to do:**

1. Run the skill for each client separately
   - For each invoice, say: "Draft reminder sequence for [Client], $[Amount], scenario: invoice goes overdue in 30 days, [relationship type]"
   - Or specify: "Prepare 4-message sequence I'll start sending at Day 15"

2. Save the generated sequences as drafts
   - Copy each 4-message sequence into your email client as drafts or a shared document
   - Label each with the client name and expected trigger date

3. Customize each sequence
   - Check that the relationship context is correctly reflected (enterprise clients often need more formal language)
   - Remove or adjust any relationship risk flags based on what you know about each account

**Expected result:**

You have 5 ready-to-send reminder sequences, customized per client, waiting in your drafts folder. When an invoice hits Day 15 overdue, you just open the draft, do a final check, and send — no writing under pressure.

**Why this works:** The skill supports proactive preparation, not just reactive follow-up. Having sequences ready removes the delay that often lets small overdue invoices become large overdue invoices.

## Tips

### Tip 1: Give the Skill Your Relationship Context — It Changes Everything

The single most impactful input is your relationship description. "Long-term trusted partner" and "new client, signed contract" produce very different sequences — even for the same invoice amount and days overdue. Don't just say "a client"; say "a client we've worked with for 3 years who always pays" or "a new client we haven't invoiced before." The more specific your context, the better the tone calibration.

**Pro tip:** If you're unsure what to say, describe how you'd feel sending this email — "I don't want to seem pushy" or "I'm starting to worry they won't pay" — and the skill will interpret that as relationship context.

### Tip 2: Include Prior Contact History for Accurate Escalation

If you've already sent a reminder or called, tell the skill. "Already sent one email, no response" shifts the starting point by one level — so you don't receive a gentle reminder when you need something firmer. Without this context, the skill defaults to the standard escalation based on days overdue, which may underestimate where you actually are.

**Pro tip:** Even vague history helps: "I mentioned the invoice in our last call" or "I saw them at an event and they said they'd look into it."

### Tip 3: Send One Message at a Time — Don't Deploy the Full Sequence at Once

The skill generates all 4 messages so you're prepared, but don't send them in bulk. Send Message 1 (or wherever you start), wait the recommended interval, then send the next *only if needed*. Many clients respond after the first or second message, making the later ones unnecessary. The goal is payment — not escalation for its own sake.

**Pro tip:** If you get a payment promise ("we'll pay by Friday"), hold the sequence. If Friday passes without payment, resume from the next message in the sequence.
